How Much Does a Grant Writer Cost for a Nonprofit?

By Holly Selwitschka· Updated June 2026· 6 min read

Most freelance grant writers charge $50–$150 per hour, a flat fee of about $1,000–$5,000+ per proposal, or a monthly retainer in the $2,000–$5,000 range. The exact cost depends on the writer's experience, the complexity of the grant, and how much research the project requires. One rule holds across all of it: an ethical grant writer never charges a percentage of the grant you win.

Key takeaways

  • Hourly: roughly $50–$150/hour, higher for specialized federal grants.
  • Per project: ~$1,000–$5,000+ for a full proposal, depending on size and complexity.
  • Retainer: commonly $2,000–$5,000/month for ongoing pipeline support.
  • Never pay commission. Charging a percentage of awards is against professional ethics and many funders' rules.

The three ways grant writers charge

1. Hourly

Hourly billing is common for smaller or one-off projects. You pay for the time spent on research, drafting, and revisions. It's transparent and flexible, and it works well when the scope is hard to predict up front. New and emerging grant writers sit at the lower end; seasoned specialists charge more.

2. Flat project fee

For a defined proposal, many writers quote a single flat fee. You know the cost before work begins, which makes budgeting easy. A short letter of inquiry costs far less than a 25-page federal application — so the range is wide, and the quote should reflect the specific grant.

3. Monthly retainer

If you want a steady stream of applications and reporting handled, a retainer buys a set amount of work each month. This suits organizations building a real grant pipeline rather than chasing one deadline at a time.

Why ethical grant writers don't work on commission

It's tempting to think "I'll just pay a percentage if we win." Don't — and be cautious of anyone who offers it. Professional bodies such as the Grant Professionals Association prohibit commission-based or contingent-fee grant writing in their code of ethics, and many funders explicitly forbid paying for proposals out of awarded funds. Contingency pricing also creates bad incentives: it pushes writers toward easy, ill-fitting grants instead of the right ones for your mission. You're paying for expertise and effort, not a lottery ticket.

What you're really paying for

A good grant writer saves your staff dozens of hours per application and submits a sharper, more competitive proposal than a stretched team can usually produce. Think of the cost as buying back your team's time and raising your odds — not as an expense detached from results. Over a year, one or two additional awards can easily outweigh the fees.

How to keep costs manageable

  • Start with a free consultation so you only pay once there's a clear fit and plan.
  • Prioritize a few well-matched grants over a scattershot of long-shots.
  • Keep a "grant-ready" file — mission statement, budget, board list, outcomes data — to cut research time.
  • Ask about capacity-building funders that support fundraising infrastructure.
